Chest & Lower Respiratory Disorders

Tuberculosis (TB)

Airborne transmission. Classic triad: low-grade fever + night sweats + weight loss, plus persistent cough and possible hemoptysis. Insidious, slow onset.

Priority on suspicionInitiate airborne isolation immediately — negative pressure room (air flows inward so contaminated air can't escape into the hallway) + N95 respirator. Obtain sputum sample BEFORE giving any medications.
  • Anti-TB meds for 6–12 months — patient is generally non-infectious after 2–3 weeks but must complete the full course
  • Postural drainage uses gravity to help clear bronchial secretions
  • Watch for depression/loneliness from prolonged isolation

Pneumothorax

TypeKey feature
Spontaneous/simpleNo trauma; may reinflate on its own; chest tube if it doesn't
TraumaticRib fracture, stab, gunshot breaching the pleura
TensionOne-way valve traps air → trachea deviates away from affected side → ↓ venous return → ↓ cardiac output → death if untreated
Tension pneumothoraxDo not wait for X-ray if suspected clinically — immediate needle decompression, then chest tube.

Pneumonia

  • Confusion in the elderly may be the only presenting sign — classic atypical presentation
  • CAP (community), aspiration (elderly, dysphagia, decreased LOC), VAP (intubated patients)
  • HOB elevated prevents aspiration; oral care reduces VAP risk; 3L fluid/day thins secretions
  • Swallow eval: small sip of water — struggling → NPO + formal swallow eval

Pleural conditions

ConditionKey feature
PleurisyPleuritic pain worsens with breathing (parietal pleura has nerve endings) — teach splinting when coughing
Pleural effusionDecreased/absent breath sounds + dull/flat percussion over the fluid
EmpyemaPus in pleural space — drain fluid + antibiotics 4–6 weeks

Chest trauma

  • Flail chest — 3+ adjacent ribs fractured in 2+ places → paradoxical chest wall movement (in on inspiration, out on expiration) → severe respiratory distress, may need intubation. Aggressive pain management is essential.
  • Hemothorax — blood in pleural cavity → trachea deviates to the unaffected side, absent breath sounds on affected side, position High-Fowler's
  • Ribs 4–10 most commonly fractured; heal in 3–6 weeks; still get an ECG to rule out cardiac injury
Tension pneumothorax vs. hemothoraxBoth cause absent breath sounds and tracheal deviation — but tension PTX deviates away from the affected side, hemothorax deviates toward the unaffected side. Differentiate by mechanism and clinical context.

PE, ARDS, and acute respiratory failure

  • PE — most often from a DVT; prevention = early ambulation, SCDs
  • ARDS — hypoxemia that does not respond to O2; bilateral infiltrates on CXR; onset within 72 hrs of trigger; needs mechanical ventilation, prone positioning helps
  • ARF criteria: PaO2 <60, PaCO2 >50, pH <7.35. Early sign = restlessness, not confusion (that's late)

Ventilator alarms & lung cancer

  • Low pressure alarm → leak/disconnection, check for extubation
  • High pressure alarm → obstruction (secretions, biting tube, kinking) → suction, reposition
  • Never turn off vent alarms
  • Lung cancer: a cough that changes in character is the classic early warning sign — leading cause of cancer death in the US
